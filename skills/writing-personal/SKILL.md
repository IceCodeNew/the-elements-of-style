# Writing personal text

These rules hold for personal writing in every language. Load the skill for the text's language too: `writing-en`, `writing-ja` or `writing-zh`.

## Rules

- When you make a general point about what people do or feel, show the reader the situation from inside: what they would do, see or find there. "You scroll past forty pages of terms and tick the box" shows what "Nobody reads the terms" only asserts. Build the scene only from what the material gives: add no times, numbers, events or feelings. Keep the claim at the strength the material gives it; a scene does not turn an impression into a fact. <!-- r:pers.reader-in-scene -->
- In an essay or blog post that readers read to the end, put the point the piece exists to make (the verdict, the answer or the open question) in the last paragraph and stop there, with no recap, moral or advice after it. A general claim about how people should live or work is such a moral, not the point of a personal story, unless the piece argues for it with its own material. In a reply to a comment or message, a social post, or a short item in a newsletter digest, state that point in the first sentence, and do not state it again where the account reaches it. <!-- r:pers.end-on-the-point -->
- When you admit a bias, a mistake or a limit, name it and say what it changes about your claim: "I grew up in this town, so I am probably too kind to its bakeries." Replace assurances such as "trust me", "I promise" or "this is not a rant" with the evidence they stand in for. <!-- r:pers.specific-admission -->
- Put a statement in positive form when a positive word states the same fact exactly: "the failing build", not "the build that isn't passing"; "common", not "not uncommon". Use "not" for instructions, warnings, limits and real negations: "does not support Windows". <!-- r:both.positive-form -->
- Give the author only the feelings, reactions, opinions, plans, lessons and advice that their notes, draft or messages contain. A first-person reaction is a claim about the author, so one the material does not show ("I've never felt prouder", "Honestly, it still stings") is invented even when it sounds likely: ask the author for it or leave it out. To make a post more personal, change how you tell what the material holds, not what it holds. <!-- r:pers.no-invented-reactions -->
- If the text replies to a message or thread that you can see, open with the decision or answer, then add only what the reader still lacks to agree or act: usually one fact and the link they need. Leave out background that the reader wrote or already agreed to, and leave the diagnosis and the proof for the ticket or document that follows. <!-- r:both.answer-first-in-replies -->
- Define a term, with its scope, before or where you first use it: say first what kind of thing it is (a data structure, a step, a limit), then its properties, and when you group several things under one name, first say in one sentence that they count as one thing. From then on, use that term for that thing in every section, with the same meaning and classification, not a synonym or a vaguer word. If you must use a second name, state once that it means the same; for an idea that is not a term, use plain words, not a word that sounds like a term. <!-- r:both.define-and-keep-terms -->
- Describe what a thing is and does instead of praising it, and keep a claim that a fact is a turning point, a legacy or a sign of things to come only with the concrete consequence that shows it: "the team's first public release", not "a new era for the team". Cut a trailing clause that only restates importance and keep one that gives a real cause, purpose or result; under a heading on challenges or outlook, name specific problems and concrete plans, and add no reassurance or prediction that the material does not support. If the writer gives a personal judgment, keep it as the writer's, in specific terms, not as a measured result; state a risk and its consequence once, without stacking consequences for effect. <!-- r:both.facts-not-praise -->
- Give each claim the certainty its evidence gives: state firmly what the material establishes, and keep a hedge for what is guessed, inferred, unverified or only planned, so that a possibility does not become a fact and a plan does not become a finished act. If one claim carries several qualifiers for the same doubt, keep one; keep scope limits, legal and safety notices, and polite softening, which do not report doubt. Example: "The patch may reduce timeouts" stays a possibility until a test shows the effect. <!-- r:both.keep-certainty -->
- If an event has several causes, or a passage covers several decisions or problems, name each one and say which fact or remedy goes with which, instead of calling them the same or covering them with one word or one cause. Example: "The outage had two causes: the certificate expired, and no alert fired", not "The outage was a monitoring failure". <!-- r:both.name-every-cause -->
- Answer only the objections and alternatives that a reader or a named source would raise or weigh, such as a rejected design option or a common mistake. Cut a defense against a charge nobody made ("I am not against testing, but..."); if the defense holds a real claim, state that claim. If an example may look contrived to the reader, say so and give brief grounds that it is realistic. <!-- r:both.no-phantom-objections -->
- Give each paragraph one topic: one step of an argument, or one stage of a story or scene. If a paragraph covers two topics or stages, such as what was found and how it was checked, split it; if a short piece has one topic, one paragraph is enough. <!-- r:both.one-topic-per-paragraph -->
- Instead of calling something important, interesting, hard, essential or thorough, give the fact that makes it so, or cut the label: name the reasons, implications or what was examined in the same sentence, and replace a verb that only claims depth or a stance with what was done. Add a caution only for a concrete risk the reader faces, and name its condition, such as which versions or regions, instead of saying that results may vary. If the sentence already says why, keep the word: "essential, because the job fails without it". <!-- r:both.show-not-label -->
- State a point instead of staging it: merge a run of fragments or one-line paragraphs that restate one point into a sentence that states it ("No cache. No retries." becomes "It ran without a cache or retries"), and replace a saying that dresses an ordinary point as a deep truth with the specific claim that the material gives, or cut it, even when it is the draft's last line; keep a short fragment that adds a new fact at the climax, and a short run that the author's draft uses as a joke or allusion. Use suspense, a rhetorical question, a question you answer yourself, a reaction you act out for the reader, or a dramatic turn only where it carries a new fact or the one climax the passage builds to, and use none of them in reference text, UI text or error messages. A one-sentence paragraph is fine for a transition or for a short self-contained entry such as a note. <!-- r:both.state-dont-stage -->
