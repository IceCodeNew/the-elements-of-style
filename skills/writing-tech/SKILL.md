# Writing technical text

These rules hold for technical text in every language. Load the skill for the text's language too: `writing-en`, `writing-ja` or `writing-zh`.

## Sentences

- Put a statement in positive form when a positive word states the same fact exactly: "the failing build", not "the build that isn't passing"; "common", not "not uncommon". Use "not" for instructions, warnings, limits and real negations: "does not support Windows". <!-- r:both.positive-form -->
- If the actor is known and the reader needs it, make the actor the subject: "The controller deletes the object." If the actor is unknown or does not matter to the reader, use the passive or a sentence without a subject, and do not invent an actor such as "the system" or "the team". Give a decision, intention or judgment to the person or group that made it, not to a version, a file, the data or "the market"; a program can still return, reject or show. <!-- r:both.name-the-actor -->
- Write each sentence so that it has one reading. Put a modifier next to the word that it modifies, and name the thing when "this", "it" or "the issue" could point to more than one thing. <!-- r:both.one-reading -->
- Cut a word, phrase or sentence if the text says the same without it: an intensifier (if the point needs emphasis, choose a word that is strong in itself), a phrase that frames a topic the sentence already names, a chain of connectives that only add, and a sentence that only links or praises, such as "This in itself is a good thing." Keep the words that carry manner, scope, time, frequency, negation, condition, cause or real doubt. Judge a cut by meaning, not by length: if it would leave the reader to infer who acts, on what, why or under which condition, keep the words. <!-- r:both.cut-needless-words -->
- If two clauses stand in a relation of cause, result, contrast, concession, order or simultaneity, name the relation with a connective instead of a link that only adds or bare juxtaposition: "The cache was cold, so the first request timed out." Name only relations that the facts state: cut a clause that only claims significance instead of recasting it as a relation, and keep a dash that explains or expands the clause before it. Open a paragraph with such a connective when its relation to the one before is not plain from the content, add none that only signals addition, and recast some of several sentences in a row that share the same loose two-clause shape. <!-- r:both.name-the-relation -->
- Keep sentences and paragraphs short, with one statement in each sentence; a command, path or identifier inside a sentence does not count toward its length. To shorten a long sentence, cut words first. Split it only where neither part has to repeat a noun phrase from the other and no causal or conditional link (because, unless, so that) is lost. <!-- r:tech.short-sentences -->

## Structure

- Open each section and each expository paragraph with its main point: what the thing is and what it does, what was decided and why, or what caused the failure. A sentence that only says what the section covers, or counts the items that follow, is not a main point: in a section of separate items, start with the first item, and do not state the point again at the end. Put background and history after the point; if a passage tells events in time order or walks through an example, keep that order. <!-- r:tech.point-first -->
- If the text replies to a message or thread that you can see, open with the decision or answer, then add only what the reader still lacks to agree or act: usually one fact and the link they need. Leave out background that the reader wrote or already agreed to, and leave the diagnosis and the proof for the ticket or document that follows. <!-- r:both.answer-first-in-replies -->
- Start with the point: cut a sentence or lead-in that only announces, teases or names the idea that comes next instead of stating it, and a first sentence under a heading that only restates the heading. Keep a sentence that states a long document's scope, order or exclusions, and a lead-in that introduces a list, table, code block or command. Describe a layout or convention only if the reader cannot see or infer it, and when you refer to a table or figure, say what to take from it: "Table 2 shows that retries double the latency." <!-- r:both.no-empty-previews -->
- State each claim once: cut a sentence or closing line that repeats what the reader already has from earlier text or the conversation, including one that only names what the example just showed, and merge sections that say the same thing from different angles; keep a sentence that adds a fact, condition, limit or consequence. Put the conclusion in one place: after the objections in an argument that builds to it, or first in a text type that leads with it, without a restatement at the end. If the text has a summary or abstract that readers may read alone, a self-contained procedure or reference entry, or a warning repeated at the step it guards, keep that repetition. <!-- r:both.say-it-once -->

## Words and names

- Use the term and translation that the field already uses (push notifications: 配信 in Japanese, 推送 in Chinese); do not coin a term or use a metaphor as a term when an established one exists. If the tool, command or UI names the operation, use that name instead of team slang: "create three temporary replicas" for `pgctl replicas create --count 3 --temporary`, not "spin up three throwaway replicas". <!-- r:tech.established-terms -->
- Name a file, function, variable or other identifier only if the reader will type it, search for it, or meet it again in the text; otherwise describe it by its role ("the config loader"). Procedures and reference entries give the exact name. <!-- r:tech.identifiers-when-used -->
- Name the exact thing that the material gives: the error text, version, role or relation instead of a broad word for it ("the tool", "is linked to"), and the fact that fits only this subject instead of a description that would fit many. Back a general claim with at least one instance, and keep the details that bear on the point; drop those that do not, such as timestamps in an example about design, names that never come back, or a persona's job title that changes nothing in the example. If the material gives no specific, stay general or ask for one. <!-- r:both.be-specific -->
- Define a term, with its scope, before or where you first use it: say first what kind of thing it is (a data structure, a step, a limit), then its properties, and when you group several things under one name, first say in one sentence that they count as one thing. From then on, use that term for that thing in every section, with the same meaning and classification, not a synonym or a vaguer word. If you must use a second name, state once that it means the same; for an idea that is not a term, use plain words, not a word that sounds like a term. <!-- r:both.define-and-keep-terms -->
- In a sentence that makes a claim, use verbs that mean literally what you claim. If a first-time reader could picture the verb's literal action (a vector that carries a preference, a model that knows a style), restate who does what to what in concrete words, such as "the model's outputs keep a consistent style"; if you cannot restate it, the sentence does not say what it refers to, so cut it or settle the content and rewrite it. Verbs for what software does, such as return, send or reject, are literal, and an image that you chose for effect in personal writing and that makes no claim may stay. <!-- r:both.literal-claims -->
- Instead of calling something important, interesting, hard, essential or thorough, give the fact that makes it so, or cut the label: name the reasons, implications or what was examined in the same sentence, and replace a verb that only claims depth or a stance with what was done. Add a caution only for a concrete risk the reader faces, and name its condition, such as which versions or regions, instead of saying that results may vary. If the sentence already says why, keep the word: "essential, because the job fails without it". <!-- r:both.show-not-label -->
- Say what a method, setting or change does as its concrete effect, with plain verbs, as far as the material states it. Replace spoken lab jargon (EN "works", "has legs"; JA 効く, 刺さる; ZH 好使, 有戏) and twisted idioms with that effect: not "the style skill works" but "with the style skill loaded, the output comes closer to what reviewers prefer". <!-- r:tech.plain-effect -->

## Change documents

- In docs, code comments and reference text, describe what the code or system does now. Mention what it replaced or how it used to work only in changelogs, release notes, migration guides, deprecation notices, commit messages and PR descriptions. <!-- r:tech.describe-current-state -->
- Cut sentences about how the text was produced ("based on the maintainers' notes", "compiled from the logs", "unconfirmed items are flagged"). Keep a source the reader can follow, and keep the methods and test setup the reader needs to judge or repeat a result. <!-- r:tech.no-production-narration -->

## Procedures and specs

- Write a procedure as numbered steps in the imperative, with one action in each step. Put the command in a code block under the step that runs it. <!-- r:tech.one-action-per-step -->
- Put a condition before the action it controls: "If `pgctl check` reports an error, run `pgctl failover --to db-2`." A reader who acts while reading must not reach the command before the condition. <!-- r:tech.condition-before-action -->
- Put a warning directly before the step it protects, not after the step or in a notes section at the end. Start with what to do or not do, then give the consequence once: "Do not skip this step. Without the backup, a failed upgrade loses every write since the last backup." <!-- r:tech.warning-placement -->
- In specs, API contracts and procedures, mark each requirement level with one fixed keyword and keep it, for example must / should / may in English (in capitals only where the document adopts RFC 2119), しなければならない / することが望ましい / してもよい (JIS Z 8301), 应 / 宜 / 可 (GB/T 1.1). Keep the level the source gives: a recommendation does not become "must". <!-- r:tech.requirement-keywords -->

## Claims and evidence

- Write as fact only what the material establishes: add no fact, number, name, date, quote, source or result that the material or the author did not give, and if a sentence needs a detail you lack, ask for it or write a simpler sentence. If the material does not show something the reader needs, say once that it is unknown or undocumented, and do not follow that with a guess written as fact; a guess you keep must read as your own guess. If you need an example value that the material does not give, use a reserved placeholder such as example.com or 192.0.2.1. <!-- r:both.claims-match-evidence -->
- Describe what a thing is and does instead of praising it, and keep a claim that a fact is a turning point, a legacy or a sign of things to come only with the concrete consequence that shows it: "the team's first public release", not "a new era for the team". Cut a trailing clause that only restates importance and keep one that gives a real cause, purpose or result; under a heading on challenges or outlook, name specific problems and concrete plans, and add no reassurance or prediction that the material does not support. If the writer gives a personal judgment, keep it as the writer's, in specific terms, not as a measured result; state a risk and its consequence once, without stacking consequences for effect. <!-- r:both.facts-not-praise -->
- Answer only the objections and alternatives that a reader or a named source would raise or weigh, such as a rejected design option or a common mistake. Cut a defense against a charge nobody made ("I am not against testing, but..."); if the defense holds a real claim, state that claim. If an example may look contrived to the reader, say so and give brief grounds that it is realistic. <!-- r:both.no-phantom-objections -->
- Make each claim as wide as its support: if your examples or data cover only part of it, narrow it to that part, and back a claim about what people do with a source, data or a case, or limit it to the group you saw: "three of the five users we watched skipped the tutorial", not "users skip tutorials". Write that something holds for all, always or never only when it literally does, and otherwise give the real scope or frequency. In specs and requirements, an absolute that states an invariant or a requirement stays. <!-- r:both.scope-claims -->
- When you claim that A leads to B and the target reader could not say why, add one sentence that gives the mechanism: "Every step reads the same data format, so a change to the format means a change to every step", not only "Splitting the work by step makes changes spread". <!-- r:both.state-the-mechanism -->
- State the status of each process, feature or change exactly as the facts give it: still running, finished, failed, planned, or available only from a given version or under a given condition. Write a sync that is still running as running ("the sync is 60% done and still running"), not as one that did not finish, and a planned feature as planned, not as available. <!-- r:tech.operation-state -->

## Lists and formatting

- Write reasoning or a story as paragraphs, and use a list for parallel, independent items that the reader scans or looks up, such as steps, options or parameters; if the labels of a labeled list only repeat their items, write the items as prose. Use a table when the reader compares several items across two or more attributes; if it would have a few rows and one value column, write a sentence or a list instead. If you have many parallel items, sort them into groups. <!-- r:both.lists-vs-prose -->
- Bold only what a reader scanning the page must find or must not miss: a warning, a negation that prevents a misreading, a term where it is defined, a section's conclusion, or UI labels where the house style bolds them; bold a term once, not every time it appears. Make other points stand out by order and structure. When you remove bold, keep the words it marked, along with any expansions, comparisons and list items. <!-- r:both.sparing-bold -->
- Give each measured value its unit, and keep one unit and number format for a quantity throughout. Put a space between a number and a unit symbol (16 GB, 250 ms, 21 °C) but none before % or the degree sign of an angle (40%, 45°), unless the house style or the existing document does otherwise, and do not convert a unit or change a value's precision to make the format uniform. <!-- r:tech.number-units -->
- Make the items of one list the same kind of thing at the same level, such as all countries or all cities but not both, in the same grammatical form, and end them alike: every item with a period if the items are full sentences, none if they are phrases. <!-- r:both.consistent-list-items -->

## Register

- Keep reference, procedure, specification and error text neutral and plain, with no jokes, exclamations or chatty asides. In design docs, postmortems and technical articles, give the author's judgment once, as a labeled recommendation or assessment that names the choice and the facts behind it: "Recommendation: use B, because it needs no schema change." When you edit formal technical or academic text, keep its formality, attributions and the qualifiers that limit a claim instead of making it conversational, but not its intensifiers, praise or stakes. <!-- r:tech.neutral-register -->
